# Reference

## observable-universe-log-map.jpg

The observable universe on a logarithmic scale, from the ground to the Big Bang: what this
simulator should hold at the least. Every object labelled on it is a target, drawn at its real
place and size.

[checklist.md](checklist.md) lists every label on it and whether the simulator has it yet.

It is an illustration, not a map. The distance up the page is logarithmic and only roughly
true; the objects are enlarged so their shapes show, and they are placed for the composition,
not where they lie on the sky. Some labels have aged:

- **HD1** is labelled the most distant known galaxy. It was a photometric candidate
  (z ≈ 13.3, 2022) and is unconfirmed. The most distant galaxies confirmed by spectra are
  JADES-GS-z14-0 (z = 14.32; Carniani et al. 2024) and MoM-z14 (z = 14.44; Naidu et al. 2025).
- **"Ereandel"** is Earendel, the most distant single star seen (z ≈ 6.2; Welch et al. 2022).
- **GRB 090429B**'s redshift, z ≈ 9.4, is photometric.
- **Tonantzintla 618**'s 66 billion solar masses is an estimate from its broad lines, the
  largest of several such claims, not a measured record.
- **ASASSN-15lh** may not be a supernova: it may have been a star torn apart by a spinning
  black hole (Leloudas et al. 2016).
- **The Kordylewski clouds** are reported in polarised light (Slíz-Balogh et al. 2018) and
  remain disputed.
- **The largest structures** it shows (the Hercules–Corona Borealis Great Wall, the Giant GRB
  Ring, the Huge-LQG) are claims; newer ones it lacks include the Giant Arc (Lopez et al. 2022),
  the Big Ring (Lopez et al. 2024) and Quipu (Böhringer et al. 2025).
